In the foundations chapter, we developed the logic of statistical inference — null and alternative hypotheses, the two types of error, the significance level, the p-value and critical region approaches to making a decision, and the idea of a confidence interval — without performing any computations. That development and reasoning do not depend on which parameter we study. In this chapter we put it to work for a population proportion \(p,\) filling in the parameter-specific machinery the foundations chapter deferred: the statistics and their sampling distribution, the calculations that produce a p-value and a critical region, and the software steps to carry them out in Rguroo. We consider both one-population and two-population settings for proportions.

Because the conceptual framework is already in place, this chapter focuses on computation. We will recall each idea briefly as we use it, with a pointer back to the foundations chapter for the full discussion, and spend our effort on how the ideas are actually carried out for a proportion. The same pattern will repeat in the next chapter for a population mean: new computations, the same framework.

We begin with hypothesis testing for one population proportion.

9.1 Hypothesis Testing: Concepts and Setup

In many real situations, we encounter numerical claims about population proportions: the proportion of teenagers who use a particular app, the proportion of adults who meet health guidelines, or the proportion of students who rely on social media for news. But how can we determine whether such claims are reasonable? When does sample evidence support a claim, and when should we question it?

To see how we might answer such questions, we will work through examples and develop the reasoning needed to evaluate claims and reach conclusions. We begin by establishing the framework and notation we will use throughout this chapter.

9.1.1 Setting the Stage: A Running Example

Suppose we want to know what proportion of students at our university prefer online classes. The population is all students at our university. The response variable is class mode preference, a categorical variable (also called a factor) that classifies each student into one of two categories: prefers online classes or does not prefer online classes. We define “prefers online classes” as a success and the other category as a failure.

In statistics, the terms success and failure do not imply that one outcome is good and the other is bad. A success simply refers to the outcome we are interested in studying, while a failure refers to the other outcome. For example, if we are studying the proportion of manufactured parts that are defective, we might define a defective part as a success. Similarly, if we are studying the proportion of patients who experience a side effect from a medication, experiencing the side effect could be defined as a success. In both cases, “success” identifies the outcome of interest, not a desirable outcome.

The parameter of interest is the population proportion, denoted by \(p\)—the fraction of all students at our university who prefer online classes. Since we cannot survey every student, we collect data from a random sample and use it to learn about \(p.\)

This example illustrates the key elements of proportion inference:

  • A population with a characteristic we want to measure
  • A categorical response variable with two outcomes (success/failure)
  • A parameter \(p\) representing the true population proportion of successes
  • A sample from which we compute a statistic to estimate \(p\)

Let’s develop this framework more formally.

9.1.2 From Samples to Statistics

When we collect a sample of size \(n,\) we count how many individuals have a success outcome. Before the sample is collected, this count is unknown, so we treat it as a random variable and denote it by \(X.\) The corresponding sample proportion is also a random variable, given by \(\hat{p}=X/n\) (read “p-hat”).

After the data are collected, we observe the count of successes in our sample, which we denote by \(x_{\text{obs}}\) (read “x-observed”). The corresponding observed sample proportion is \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}=\frac{x_{\text{obs}}}{n}\) (read “p-hat-observed”) which provides an estimate of the population proportion \(p.\)

In our example of students who prefer online classes, suppose we randomly select \(n = 100\) students. Before we collect the sample, \(X\) represents the number of students who will say they prefer online classes in a random sample of \(n=100\) students. The value of \(X\) could be anywhere from 0 to 100. Correspondingly, \(\hat{p}\) represents the proportion of students in the sample who say that they prefer online classes. After we survey the students, suppose 62 say they prefer online classes. Then, the observed count is \(x_{\text{obs}} = 62\) and the observed proportion is \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} = 62/100 = 0.62\) or 62%. .

\(X\) is a random variable representing the number of successes across all possible samples; \(x_{\text{obs}}\) is the specific count from our actual sample. Similarly, \(\hat{p}=X/n\) is a random variable representing the sample proportion across all possible samples, while \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}=x_{\text{obs}}/n\) is the observed sample proportion from our actual sample. We use \(x_{\text{obs}},\) or equivalently \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}},\) to make decisions about \(p.\)

Notation Summary

As we work through the examples in this chapter, it may be helpful to keep the following symbols and their meanings in mind.

Symbol Name Description
\(p\) Population proportion The true, unknown proportion in the population
\(n\) Sample size The number of individuals in the sample
\(X\) Random variable (count) The number of successes across all possible samples of size \(n\)
\(x_{\text{obs}}\) Observed count The actual number of successes in our sample
\(\hat{p}\) Random variable (proportion) The sample proportion \(X/n\) across all possible samples of size \(n\)
\(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) Observed sample proportion The proportion of success we observed in our sample; estimate of \(p\) computed as \(x_{\text{obs}}/n\)

To use sample evidence to evaluate a claim about a population proportion, we first need to state clearly the claims we are considering. Because our decision is based on a sample rather than the entire population, there is always a possibility of reaching the wrong conclusion. As we saw in Chapter 8, we might reject a claim that is actually true, or we might fail to reject a claim that is actually false. These two possibilities correspond to Type I and Type II errors, which we will now consider in the context of inference for a population proportion.

9.1.3 Null and Alternative Hypotheses and Errors for a Proportion

The foundations chapter (Section 8.3) developed the null and alternative hypotheses, the three forms they take, and the two types of error in full. Here, we briefly review the notation for hypothesis tests involving a population proportion and then apply these ideas to our examples.

For a population proportion, the alternative hypothesis \(H_a\) is the claim we seek evidence for, and the null hypothesis \(H_0\) is its complement, the value or range we assume true unless the data force us to abandon it. Both are statements about the population proportion \(p,\) not about the sample, so they are written using \(p\) and not \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}.\) Writing \(p_0\) for the boundary value that separates the null from the alternative, the three forms are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Case 1: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p \leq p_0 \\ H_a: p > p_0 \end{cases} \qquad \text{Case 2: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p \geq p_0 \\ H_a: p < p_0 \end{cases} \\[1em] \text{Case 3: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p = p_0 \\ H_a: p \neq p_0 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \] Cases 1 and 2 are one-sided and Case 3 is two-sided, exactly as in Section 8.3.1.

Recall too the two ways a test can be wrong: a Type I error rejects \(H_0\) when \(H_0\) is in fact true, and a Type II error fails to reject \(H_0\) when \(H_a\) is in fact true (see Section 8.4.1). The following examples write the hypotheses and interpret both errors for our three proportion scenarios.

9.2 Making a Decision about a Population Proportion

For a two-sided test, an observed sample proportion either above or below the null value can provide evidence against \(H_0.\) For example, in the social media example with \(H_0: p = 0.36\) versus \(H_a: p \neq 0.36,\) a sample proportion that differs from \(0.36\) in either direction could provide evidence in favor of \(H_a.\) However, simply observing that \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) differs from \(0.36\) is not enough to reject \(H_0.\) Some difference is expected because of random sampling variation. We need to perform a hypothesis test to determine whether the observed difference is large enough to provide sufficient evidence against \(H_0.\)

For a one-sided test, there is an additional consideration: the observed sample proportion must first be in the direction specified by \(H_a\) to provide evidence against \(H_0.\) For instance, in Example 9.1, the Daily YouTube Use Among Teens example, the hypotheses are \(H_0: p \leq 0.70\) versus \(H_a: p > 0.70.\) If a sample of \(n = 20\) students resulted in only \(x_{\text{obs}} = 12\) students reporting daily use, then \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} = \frac{12}{20} = 0.60.\) Since \(0.60\) falls within the values specified by \(H_0\) rather than in the direction of \(H_a,\) this sample result clearly does not provide evidence against \(H_0\) in favor of \(H_a.\)

On the other hand, if \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) were greater than \(0.70,\) being in the direction of \(H_a\) would not by itself be enough to reject \(H_0.\) We would still need to perform the hypothesis test to determine whether the observed result is sufficiently far from the null value to be unlikely to occur by random chance, assuming \(H_0\) is true.

Now consider sample results that point in the direction of \(H_a.\) For the YouTube example (\(H_a: p > 0.70\)), \(x_{\text{obs}} = 17\) out of \(n = 20\) gives \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} = 0.85,\) which is greater than \(0.70.\) For the physical inactivity example (\(H_a: p < 0.35\)), \(x_{\text{obs}} = 10\) out of \(n = 50\) gives \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} = 0.20,\) which is less than \(0.35.\) For the social media example (\(H_a: p \neq 0.36\)), \(x_{\text{obs}} = 9\) out of \(n = 15\) gives \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} = 0.60,\) which is noticeably different from \(0.36.\) Each of these results points in the direction of \(H_a.\) But is that enough to reject \(H_0\)?

Not necessarily!

Consistency With \(H_a\) Is Not the Same as Strong Evidence in Favor of \(H_a\)

A sample result that points in the direction of \(H_a\) does not by itself constitute strong evidence in favor of \(H_a.\) Because of sampling variability, such a result can occur by chance even when \(H_0\) is true. The key question is whether the sample result would be unlikely to occur by chance if \(H_0\) were true. If so, we have strong evidence against \(H_0\) and in favor of \(H_a.\)

We now turn to actually carrying out a hypothesis test for a proportion. Recall from Section 8.4.2 that, because reducing one type of error tends to increase the other, we control the probability of a Type I error by holding it to a small, prespecified significance level \(\alpha.\) Recall also that there are two equivalent ways to reach a decision: the p-value approach and the critical region approach. These approaches always lead to the same decision.

There are two methods we will use to carry out a hypothesis test for one population proportion: a large-sample approach based on the normal distribution and an exact approach based on the binomial distribution. We begin with the large-sample approach in Section 9.3, which relies on the central limit theorem and requires a sufficiently large sample for the normal approximation to work well. We then consider the exact binomial approach in Section 9.4, which uses the binomial distribution directly and does not require a large sample.

It is important to distinguish these two methods from the p-value and critical region approaches. The large-sample and exact binomial methods determine how we calculate the probabilities for the test. The p-value and critical region approaches determine how we use those probabilities to make a decision about \(H_0.\)

The exact binomial method is an ideal choice when we want to avoid relying on a large-sample approximation because it uses the binomial distribution directly to calculate exact probabilities. It does not require a large sample for the normal approximation to be valid. Nevertheless, there are good reasons to begin with the large-sample approach. Most importantly, the basic ideas generalize naturally to other inference problems, such as comparing two population proportions and drawing inferences about population means. In addition, large-sample methods have historically been widely used, so you will frequently encounter them in published research. Finally, because the large-sample approach builds on the familiar normal distribution, it provides an accessible starting point for learning how hypothesis tests are carried out.

9.3 Large Sample Hypothesis Testing

The large sample approach rests on the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), which you have already seen in Chapter 7. Recall that the CLT tells us that, for a sufficiently large sample, the sampling distribution of a sample mean is approximately normal, even when the population distribution itself is not normal. At first, this may seem unrelated to proportions: the CLT concerns sample means, while our interest here is in a sample proportion. However, as we will see next, a sample proportion can itself be viewed as a sample mean. This connection allows us to apply the CLT directly to sample proportions and provides the foundation for the large-sample approach.

9.3.1 A Sample Proportion Is a Mean

Suppose we record whether each individual in our sample has the characteristic of interest by assigning a value of 1 to each success and a value of 0 to each failure. For example, in the YouTube study (Example 9.1), a student who uses YouTube daily receives a 1, and a student who does not receives a 0. If our sample of \(n = 20\) students produced 17 daily users, the data would consist of 17 ones and 3 zeros. The sum of these 20 values is 17, and their average is

\[ \frac{17}{20} = 0.85, \]

which is exactly the observed sample proportion, \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}.\)

More generally, if a sample of size \(n\) produces some number of successes, then

\[ \hat{p}_{\text{obs}} = \frac{\text{number of successes}}{n} = \frac{\text{sum of the 0/1 values}}{n}. \]

Thus, when successes and failures are coded as 1 and 0, the observed sample proportion is simply the mean of the zeros and ones in the sample.

The same idea applies to any random sample of size \(n.\) For each possible sample, the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\) is the mean of its 0/1 values. Thus, a sample proportion is a special case of a sample mean: it is the mean of observations that can take only the values 0 and 1.

This connection allows us to apply the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) to sample proportions. Recall that the CLT tells us that, for a sufficiently large sample size, the sampling distribution of a sample mean is approximately normal. Since a sample proportion is a sample mean based on 0/1 observations, its sampling distribution is also approximately normal for a sufficiently large sample.

9.3.2 Applying the Central Limit Theorem to \(\hat p\)

Since \(\hat{p}\) is a sample mean of 0s and 1s, its sampling distribution follows the same general pattern as other sample means. For a population with proportion of successes \(p,\) the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) has:

  • Mean: \(\mu_{\hat{p}}=p\)
  • Standard deviation: \(\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\)

This standard deviation is often called the standard error of \(\hat{p}.\) It measures how much we expect \(\hat{p}\) to vary from sample to sample. By the CLT, when \(n\) is sufficiently large, \(\hat{p}\) follows approximately a normal distribution:

\[ \hat{p} \sim N\!\left(p,\; \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\right). \]

Since \(p\) is unknown, we need to specify a value for it to use this result for inference. Recall that in hypothesis testing we assume the null hypothesis is true, and that the maximum Type I error occurs at the boundary value \(p_0\) of \(H_0\) (see Section 8.3.2). We therefore evaluate the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) at \(p = p_0,\) giving:

\[ \hat{p} \sim N\!\left(p_0,\; \sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}\right). \]

We call this the null distribution of \(\hat{p},\) since it describes the behavior of \(\hat{p}\) at the boundary value \(p_0\) of the null hypothesis \(H_0,\) where the Type I error is at its maximum. This normal null distribution of \(\hat{p}\) is the foundation for decisions in hypothesis testing in the large sample method.

An option is to use a standardized statistic called the \(z\)-score, obtained by subtracting \(p_0\) from \(\hat{p}\) and dividing by the standard error:

\[ z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}. \]

This unit-less quantity is then compared to a standard normal distribution. In this textbook, we instead work directly with \(\hat{p}\) and compare it to its null distribution. This requires fewer computational steps and, more importantly, keeps our decisions on the original scale — conclusions are about a sample proportion rather than an abstract standardized value.

9.3.3 What is Considered a Large Sample Size?

What constitutes a large sample size for the normal approximation to hold? A common rule of thumb is to check the expected numbers of successes and failures. In particular, the approximation is typically considered reliable when \(np_0 \geq 10\) and \(n(1 - p_0) \geq 10.\) When both of these conditions are satisfied, the binomial distribution tends to be reasonably symmetric, and the normal model provides a good fit.

Large Sample Condition: The 10-10 Rule

The normal approximation for \(\hat{p}\) is considered reliable when both of the following hold:

\[np_0 \geq 10 \qquad \text{and} \qquad n(1-p_0) \geq 10.\]

These conditions ensure that the sample is large enough for the CLT to provide a good approximation. If either condition fails, the exact binomial method (see Section 9.4.2 and Section 9.4.3) is the appropriate choice.

To illustrate the 10–10 rule, consider the YouTube Daily Use example, where \(n=20\) and \(p_0=0.70\):

  • Expected successes: \(np_0 = 20\times 0.70 = 14\)
  • Expected failures: \(n(1-p_0) = 20\times 0.30 = 6\)

Since the expected number of failures is less than 10, the 10–10 rule is not satisfied. Therefore, the normal approximation may not be reliable for this example.

In contrast, for the Physical Inactivity example, where \(n=50\) and \(p_0=0.35\):

  • Expected successes: \(np_0 = 50\times 0.35 = 17.5\)
  • Expected failures: \(n(1-p_0) = 50\times 0.65 = 32.5\)

Since both values exceed 10, the 10–10 rule is satisfied. Therefore, the normal approximation is appropriate for the Physical Inactivity example.

9.3.4 Decision Based on a p-value

We explained the idea behind the p-value approach in Section 8.5. In this section we show how p-values are computed using the large sample method for a population proportion.

To compute a p-value, we first need to identify which possible sample results provide at least as much support for \(H_a\) as the result we actually observed. For this purpose, we define what we call the \(H_a\) support.

The \(H_a\) support is the set of all possible values of the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\) that are at least as favorable to \(H_a\) as the observed sample proportion \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}.\)

For one-sided tests, this is straightforward: as shown in Figure 9.1, if \(H_a: p > p_0,\) larger values of \(\hat{p}\) are more favorable to \(H_a,\) so the \(H_a\) support consists of \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) and all values above it. Symmetrically, for \(H_a: p < p_0,\) the \(H_a\) support consists of \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) and all values below it.

For the two-sided test \(H_a: p \neq p_0,\) the \(H_a\) support consists of values in both tails of the null distribution that are at least the same distance \(d\) from \(p_0\) as \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) (see Figure 9.2). Because the normal null distribution is symmetric about \(p_0,\) these values are symmetrically positioned about \(p_0\) . If \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} > p_0,\) the \(H_a\) support is the set of values \(\hat{p} \geq \hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) together with their mirror image relative to \(p_0\) in the left tail. If \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} < p_0,\) the \(H_a\) support is the set of values \(\hat{p} \leq \hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) together with their mirror image relative to \(p_0\) in the right tail.

Once the \(H_a\) support has been identified, the p-value is the probability, assuming \(H_0\) is true, that \(\hat{p}\) falls in the \(H_a\) support. For the large-sample method, we compute this probability using the normal null distribution at the boundary value \(p=p_0.\) Thus, \[ \text{p-value} = P(\hat{p} \text{ is in the } H_a \text{ support}), \text{ assuming that } p=p_0. \] Recall from Section 8.3.2 why we use the boundary value \(p=p_0.\) A more detailed explanation is provided in the optional Section 9.5.

9.3.5 Decision Based on a Critical Region

We now turn to the critical region approach, the other standard way to reach a decision. Recall from Section 8.6 that the critical region is the set of values of the statistic for which we reject \(H_0\) in favor of \(H_a.\) Whereas the p-value approach summarizes the evidence with a probability computed from the observed proportion \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}},\) the critical region approach decides in advance which values of \(\hat{p}\) are extreme enough to reject \(H_0,\) and then simply checks whether \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) falls in the critical region.

The decision is governed by a decision rule: a rule that specifies which values of \(\hat{p}\) lead to rejecting \(H_0.\) For a one-sided test with \(H_a: p > p_0,\) large values of \(\hat{p}\) favor the alternative hypothesis, so the critical region lies in the right tail of the null distribution. To determine the critical region, we use the distribution of \(\hat{p}\) under the null hypothesis \(p=p_0,\) namely \[ \hat{p}\sim N\left(p_0, \sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}\right). \] We choose the critical value so that the probability of falling in the critical region when \(H_0\) is true equals the significance level \(\alpha.\) The shape of the critical region — right tail, left tail, or both — depends on the alternative hypothesis, as shown in Figure 9.12.

For the right-tailed test, we find the critical value \(c\) so that there is probability \(\alpha\) in the right tail: \[ P(\hat{p} \geq c)=\alpha. \] We then reject \(H_0\) if the observed sample proportion satisfies \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \geq c.\)

Similarly, for a one-sided test with \(H_a: p < p_0,\) small values of \(\hat{p}\) favor the alternative hypothesis, so the critical region lies in the left tail. We find \(c\) such that \[ P(\hat{p} \leq c)=\alpha, \] and reject \(H_0\) if \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \leq c.\)

For a two-sided test with \(H_a: p \neq p_0,\) both unusually small and unusually large values of \(\hat{p}\) favor the alternative hypothesis. The critical region therefore lies in both tails. We find lower and upper critical values, \(c_1\) and \(c_2,\) that leave probability \(\alpha/2\) in each tail: \[ P\left(\hat{p} \leq c_1 \right)=\frac{\alpha}{2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad P\left(\hat{p} \geq c_2 \right)=\frac{\alpha}{2}. \] We reject \(H_0\) if \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \leq c_1\) or \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \geq c_2.\)

9.4 Exact Binomial Hypothesis Testing

In the previous section, we used the large-sample method to carry out a hypothesis test for a population proportion. This method uses the normal distribution to approximate the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) and works well when the sample is sufficiently large. We now consider the exact binomial method, which uses the binomial distribution directly and therefore does not require a large-sample approximation. The basic reasoning of the hypothesis test remains the same: we determine how unusual the observed sample result would be if \(H_0\) were true and use either the p-value or critical region approach to make a decision. The main difference is the statistic we use. With the large-sample method, we worked with the sample proportion \(\hat{p}.\) With the exact binomial method, we work directly with \(X,\) the number of successes in the sample. Since \(\hat{p}=X/n,\) these two statistics contain the same information; we are simply expressing the sample result in a different way.

9.4.1 The Distribution of \(X\)

To use \(X\) for inference, we need to know its probability distribution. Since we are sampling from a population and counting successes, the binomial distribution provides the appropriate model. Recall from ?sec-binomial-probability-distribution that a random variable follows a binomial distribution when:

  • The number of trials \(n\) is fixed
  • Each trial results in one of two outcomes: success or failure
  • The probability of success \(p\) is the same for every trial
  • The trials are independent of one another

In our sampling context, each individual in the sample either has the characteristic of interest (success) or does not (failure). Since the sample is selected randomly, the trials are practically independent. The probability of success \(p\) is constant—it equals the population proportion. (Strictly speaking, sampling without replacement causes \(p\) to change slightly with each draw, but when the sample is small relative to the population, this change is negligible and we treat \(p\) as constant.)

Therefore, \[ X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p). \]

This distribution will be the foundation for computing probabilities and making decisions about \(p.\)

9.4.2 Decision Based on a p-Value

The p-value approach measures how unusual the observed count \(x_{\text{obs}}\) would be if the null hypothesis were true, and rejects \(H_0\) when the data are sufficiently unusual under \(H_0.\) To make this concrete for a proportion, we evaluate probabilities at the boundary value \(p_0\) (see Section 8.3.2), using the binomial distribution of the count \(X.\)

Using the definition of \(H_a\) support from Section 8.5, the \(H_a\) support for the exact binomial method consists of the values of \(X\) that are at least as supportive of \(H_a\) as the observed value \(x_{\text{obs}}.\) The p-value then is the probability, assuming \(p=p_0,\) that \(X\) takes a value in the \(H_a\) support:

\[ \text{p-value} = P(X \text{ is a value in the } H_a \text{ support), assuming } p = p_0. \]

A small p-value is evidence against \(H_0.\)

What counts as “the \(H_a\) support” depends on the form of the alternative hypothesis.

Case 1: \(H_a: p > p_0\) (e.g., \(H_a: p > 0.70\))

Larger values of \(X\) are more supportive of \(H_a.\) The \(H_a\) support consists of \(x_{\text{obs}}\) and all values larger than \(x_{\text{obs}},\) so the p-value is \[ \text{p-value} = P(X \geq x_{\text{obs}}), \text{ assuming } p = p_0. \]

To understand Case 1, note that a larger value of \(X\) means more successes in the sample, which corresponds to a higher sample proportion \(\hat{p}.\) A higher sample proportion is less consistent with \(H_0\) and more supportive of the claim that the true population proportion exceeds \(p_0.\) For example, in the YouTube example where \(x_{\text{obs}} = 17\) out of \(n = 20,\) the \(H_a\) support consists of the values \(\{17, 18, 19, 20\}\) — the observed count and every larger possibility.

Case 2: \(H_a: p < p_0\) (e.g., \(H_a: p < 0.35\))

Smaller values of \(X\) are more supportive of \(H_a.\) The \(H_a\) support consists of \(x_{\text{obs}}\) and all values smaller than \(x_{\text{obs}},\) so the p-value is \[ \text{p-value} = P(X \leq x_{\text{obs}}), \text{ assuming } p = p_0. \]

Case 2 is the mirror image of Case 1: a smaller value of \(X\) means fewer successes in the sample, which corresponds to a lower sample proportion \(\hat{p}.\) A lower sample proportion is less consistent with \(H_0\) and more supportive of the claim that the true population proportion is less than \(p_0.\) For example, in the Physical Inactivity example where \(x_{\text{obs}} = 10\) out of \(n = 50,\) the \(H_a\) support is \(\{0, 1, 2, \ldots, 9, 10\}\) — the observed count and every smaller possibility.

Case 3: \(H_a: p \neq p_0\) (e.g., \(H_a: p \neq 0.36\))

A value \(x\) is in the \(H_a\) support if it is at least as unlikely as the observed value \(x_{\text{obs}}\) under \(H_0,\) that is, \[ P(X = x) \leq P(X = x_{\text{obs}}), \text{ assuming } p = p_0. \]

Such values can occur in either tail of the distribution.

The two-sided case is easiest to see in a picture. For the Social Media News example with \(x_{\text{obs}} = 9\) out of \(n = 15\) and \(p_0 = 0.36,\) Figure 9.17 shows the binomial distribution of \(X\) under \(H_0,\) where \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(15, 0.36).\)

The \(X\) values corresponding to the orange bars are the values of \(X\) whose probability under \(H_0\) is at most equal to \(P(X = 9).\) Therefore, the \(H_a\) support appears in both tails: large values \(9, 10, 11, \ldots\) on the right and small values \(0, 1\) on the left. The p-value is the total probability of the orange bars.

The decision rule using the p-value is the same as the one introduced in Section 8.5 and used with the large-sample method: we reject \(H_0\) when the p-value is less than or equal to the significance level \(\alpha.\) The interpretation is also the same. A small p-value means that the observed result and results at least as supportive of \(H_a\) would be unlikely if \(H_0\) were true. The difference is that with the exact binomial method, we express the observed result as the number of successes \(x_{\text{obs}}\) and calculate the p-value directly from the binomial distribution of \(X.\)

Decision Rule Using the p-Value

When testing at significance level \(\alpha,\) \[ \text{If p-value} \leq \alpha, \text{ reject } H_0. \qquad \text{If p-value} > \alpha, \text{ fail to reject } H_0. \]

9.4.3 Decision Based on a Critical Region

Recall from Section 8.6 that the critical region approach identifies, in advance, the values of the statistic that lead us to reject \(H_0.\) We used this same approach with the large-sample method. For the exact binomial method, the reasoning is unchanged; the difference is that the critical region is now defined in terms of the possible values of \(X,\) the number of successes. As before, the location of the critical region is determined by the direction of the alternative hypothesis \(H_a\):

\[\text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if } \begin{cases} X \geq c & \text{when } H_a: p > p_0 \\ X \leq c & \text{when } H_a: p < p_0 \\ X \leq c_1 \text{ or } X \geq c_2 & \text{when } H_a: p \neq p_0 \end{cases}\] The values \(c,\) \(c_1,\) and \(c_2\) are called critical values, and the corresponding values of \(X\) form the critical region (also called the rejection region).

To find the critical values, we use the null distribution \[ X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p_0). \] We choose the largest critical region for which the probability of a Type I error does not exceed the significance level \(\alpha.\)

Sometimes the Actual Significance Level Is Less Than \(\alpha\)

Because the binomial distribution is discrete, \(X\) takes only whole-number values. As a result, changing a critical value causes the probability of the critical region to change in steps rather than continuously. Therefore, we often cannot find a critical region with a probability exactly equal to \(\alpha.\)

Rguroo returns the largest critical region whose probability under \(H_0\) is at or below \(\alpha.\) The probability of this critical region is the actual significance level of the test and is reported as the Exact Significance Level in the Rguroo output.

For example, if we choose \(\alpha = 0.05,\) Rguroo might return a critical region with an actual significance level \(0.0476\) or \(0.0342.\) In either case, the probability of a Type I error is below the chosen significance level of \(0.05.\)

In practice, we use Rguroo’s One-Population Proportion inference function to find this critical region.

9.5 A Closer Look at Critical Regions and p-values (Optional)

Note

This section is optional. The material here is not required for performing hypothesis tests, but it is highly recommended for readers who want a firmer conceptual grasp of what is happening behind the procedures.

In the previous sections, you learned two equivalent approaches for testing a hypothesis about a population proportion: the critical region approach and the p-value approach. In both approaches, we computed probabilities for our decisions using the boundary value of the null hypothesis, and we relied on Rguroo’s Proportion Infrence function to produce the critical region or the p-value automatically.

This section is intended to deepen your understanding of why these procedures work the way they do. Specifically, we address two questions:

  1. Why do we use the boundary value of \(H_0\) when computing probabilities required for our decisions? The null hypothesis in many tests is a statement such as \(H_0: p \leq p_0\) or \(H_0: p \geq p_0,\) which encompasses many possible values of \(p.\) Yet we always use the single boundary value \(p_0\) in our calculations. There is a principled reason for this, and understanding it clarifies what a hypothesis test is really doing.

  2. How are critical regions and p-values actually obtained from a probability distribution? Rguroo’s Proportion Inference function returns these quantities directly, but doing so hides the underlying probability calculation. By using Rguroo’s probability calculator instead, we can see exactly which area under a sampling distribution corresponds to the critical region or the p-value.

9.5.1 Why Use the Boundary Value in \(H_0\)? **

In this section we explain why we use the boundary value of \(H_0\) when computing the probability of a Type I error. The front-half discussion stated this as a fact: for a composite \(H_0\) such as \(H_0: p \leq p_0,\) the maximum Type I error probability over the values allowed by \(H_0\) occurs at the boundary \(p_0,\) so controlling the error there automatically controls it everywhere in \(H_0.\) Here we demonstrate this empirically: we compute the probability of a Type I error for several specific values of \(p\) within \(H_0,\) for one decision rule, and observe that the maximum occurs at the boundary. A formal account, including worked examples and the underlying mathematics, is given in Jamshidian and Jamshidian (2026).

9.5.2.1 Using Inverse Probability to Find the Critical Region

When using a probability calculator, we could find a critical region by trying different candidate decision rules and checking their Type I error probabilities one at a time, as illustrated in Figure 9.40. For example, we might check whether the rule “\(X \ge 17\)” has a Type I error probability close to 0.05, then try “\(X \ge 18,\)” and so on. While this trial-and-error approach works, there is a more direct method.

Many calculators provide an inverse probability function. A probability function starts with a value of \(X\) and returns the corresponding probability. An inverse probability function works in the opposite direction: we provide a probability, such as \(\alpha=0.05,\) and it returns the value of \(X\) that creates that probability. This allows us to find the rejection-region boundary directly rather than testing several possible decision rules one at a time.

To find the critical region, we use the binomial distribution with the boundary value of \(p\) from \(H_0\) as the probability of success and \(n\) as the number of trials. Instead of providing a value of \(X\) and asking for a probability, we provide the significance level \(\alpha\) as the probability. The inverse probability function then returns the corresponding boundary value of \(X,\) which we use to determine the critical region. Below, we show how this is done in Rguroo using its built-in probability calculator.

9.6 Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion

Recall from Section 8.7 that a confidence interval gives us a range of plausible values for a population parameter. In this section, our parameter is the population proportion \(p,\) and we use the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\) as our estimate of it. Because \(\hat{p}\) varies from sample to sample, we do not expect it to equal \(p\) exactly. Instead, we use the observed sample proportion \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) to construct an interval of plausible values for \(p.\)

As discussed in Section 8.7, the confidence level describes the long-run success rate of the method used to construct the interval. For example, a 95% confidence interval comes from a method that captures the true value of \(p\) in about 95% of repeated samples. In practice, however, we collect one sample and construct one interval.

Confidence Level

Imagine that we repeatedly take random samples from the same population and construct a confidence interval for \(p\) using the same method each time. Because the samples differ, the intervals will also differ. Some intervals will contain the true population proportion \(p,\) while others will not.

The confidence level tells us the percentage of these intervals that we expect to contain the true value of \(p.\) For example, a 95% confidence level means that, over many repeated samples, about 95% of the intervals constructed using this method would contain the true population proportion \(p.\)

Recall from Section 8.7 that we can construct a confidence interval by inverting a two-sided hypothesis test. For a population proportion, we consider different candidate values \(p_0\) and test \(H_0: p=p_0\) versus \(H_a:p\neq p_0\).

If we fail to reject \(H_0,\) our data are consistent with \(p_0,\) so we consider \(p_0\) a plausible value for \(p.\) If we reject \(H_0,\) we do not consider \(p_0\) plausible. We repeat this process for different values of \(p_0.\) The collection of all values for which we fail to reject \(H_0\) forms our confidence interval.

Confidence Interval by Test Inversion

A \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p\) consists of all candidate values \(p_0\) for which we fail to reject

\[ H_0:p=p_0 \]

in a two-sided hypothesis test at significance level \(\alpha.\)

The following example illustrates this idea by testing a grid of values of \(p_0\) and identifying which ones are consistent with the observed data.

This example illustrates that the choice of method for constructing a confidence interval matters. In the subsections that follow, we study two methods in detail. We begin in Section 9.6.1 with the large-sample method, which uses the normal approximation and produces a confidence interval that is symmetric around \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}.\) We consider this method first because it is widely used in practice and builds directly on the large-sample methods we have already developed for hypothesis testing. When the large-sample condition is satisfied, the normal approximation generally works well; however, it may be misleading when the condition is not satisfied, as in this example.

We then consider the exact binomial method in Section 9.6.2. This method does not rely on the normal approximation and can produce an asymmetric confidence interval. It is particularly useful when the large-sample condition is not satisfied.

Symmetry of the Normal-Approximation Interval

Because the normal distribution is symmetric, the large-sample confidence interval we will construct is symmetric around \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}.\) In contrast, an exact binomial confidence interval does not need to be symmetric. This difference can be especially noticeable when the sample size is small or \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) is close to 0 or 1.

9.6.1 Confidence Interval Based on the Large Sample Normal Approximation

We begin with the large-sample method for constructing a confidence interval for a population proportion. This method uses the normal approximation to the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) and is one of the most commonly used methods for confidence intervals for a proportion. It also builds directly on the large-sample methods we used earlier for hypothesis testing.

Recall from Section 9.3 that when the large sample condition is satisfied, the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) is approximately normal. We can use this normal distribution to determine how far we should extend on either side of the observed sample proportion \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) to obtain a range of plausible values for \(p.\)

To construct the interval, we first need to estimate the variability of \(\hat{p}.\) Recall that the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) is \[\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.\] Because the population proportion \(p\) is unknown, we use the observed sample proportion \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) in its place. The resulting estimate of the standard deviation is called the standard error of \(\hat{p}\): \[\text{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}(1 - \hat{p}_{\text{obs}})}{n}}.\]

For a confidence interval, we check the large sample condition using \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\): \[n\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \geq 10 \qquad \text{and} \qquad n(1-\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}) \geq 10.\]

When this condition is satisfied, the \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p\) takes the form

\[\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \pm z^* \times \text{SE}(\hat{p}),\]

where \(z^*\) is the critical value from the standard normal distribution corresponding to the desired confidence level.

The quantity \(z^* \times \text{SE}(\hat{p})\) is called the margin of error. It tells us how far we extend below and above \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) when constructing the interval. A larger margin of error produces a wider interval and reflects greater uncertainty in our estimate of \(p.\)

Common critical values are:

Confidence Level \(\alpha\) \(z^*\)
90% 0.10 1.645
95% 0.05 1.960
99% 0.01 2.576
Large Sample Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion

When the large sample condition \(n\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \geq 10\) and \(n(1-\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}) \geq 10\) is satisfied, an approximate \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p\) is:

\[\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \pm \underbrace{z^* \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}(1-\hat{p}_{\text{obs}})}{n}}}_{\text{margin of error}},\]

which gives the interval

\[\left(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} - z^* \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}(1-\hat{p}_{\text{obs}})}{n}},\; \hat{p}_{\text{obs}} + z^* \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}(1-\hat{p}_{\text{obs}})}{n}}\right).\]

This interval is always symmetric around \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}.\) When the large sample condition is satisfied, the normal approximation generally works well; when it is not, the resulting confidence interval may be misleading.

9.6.2 Confidence Interval Based on the Exact Binomial Method

As we saw in Example 9.28, we can construct a confidence interval for the population proportion \(p\) by testing a grid of null hypotheses \(H_0: p = p_0\) and collecting all values of \(p_0\) for which we fail to reject \(H_0.\) When this approach uses the exact binomial distribution to carry out each test, the resulting interval is called the exact binomial confidence interval. As we noted in that example, this method has an important advantage: it correctly reflects any asymmetry in the data, which can occur when the large sample condition fails and the binomial distribution is skewed.

Of course, testing every conceivable value of \(p_0\) between 0 and 1 is not practical. 9.7 Hypothesis Testing for Two Proportions

Many important research questions require comparing proportions between two groups. In medical research, clinical trials compare treatment outcomes between patients receiving a new therapy and those receiving a placebo or standard care. In the social sciences, researchers investigate differences between demographic groups. In educational research, teachers evaluate whether new instructional methods outperform traditional approaches. Understanding how to compare two population proportions allows us to evaluate whether observed differences in sample data reflect genuine population differences or are due to chance variation.

Consider a few recent examples: A Phase 3 trial of the mRNA-1273 vaccine (Moderna) with over 30,000 participants found that 0.4% of the vaccinated group developed symptomatic COVID-19 compared to 4.9% in the placebo group—a substantial difference. A 2022 study examining 1,166 articles in Political Psychology found that 35.1% of lead authors were women compared to 64.9% men, highlighting a gender gap in academic publishing. In each case, researchers collected data from two independent samples, observed different sample proportions, and found statistical evidence that the population proportions differed.

While these large-scale studies demonstrate striking differences, many research questions involve smaller samples and more subtle effects where the statistical evidence is less clear-cut. In this section, we develop the concepts and tools for comparing two population proportions by working with examples where the answer is not immediately obvious and careful statistical reasoning is required.

We extend the hypothesis testing framework from one proportion to two proportions. The same core concepts—null and alternative hypotheses, test statistics, p-values, Type I and Type II errors, and significance levels—continue to apply. However, we now focus on the difference between two population proportions rather than a single proportion.

9.7.1 The Null and Alternative Hypotheses

To develop these concepts and tools, we’ll work with four examples: an educational intervention comparing instructional approaches for teaching proportional reasoning, a health behavior study evaluating text message reminders for medication adherence, a workplace wellness program assessing nicotine patches for smoking cessation, and an analysis of gender differences in attitudes toward marijuana legalization from the General Social Survey.

When comparing two population proportions, we focus on the difference between them. We have two populations—let’s call one population 1 and the other population 2. It does not matter which population you designate as population 1 and which as population 2, as long as you clearly define your choice. We denote the population proportion for population 1 by \(p_1\) and the population proportion for population 2 by \(p_2.\) Our goal is to make inferences about the difference between these proportions: \(p_1 - p_2.\) To do this, we begin by formulating hypotheses about this difference.

Just as in the one-population case, we can formulate both one-sided and two-sided tests. The three cases are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Case 1: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p_1 \leq p_2 \\ H_a: p_1 > p_2 \end{cases} \qquad \text{Case 2: } \begin{cases} H_0: p_1 \geq p_2 \\ H_a: p_1 < p_2 \end{cases} \\ \\ \text{Case 3: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p_1 = p_2 \\ H_a: p_1 \neq p_2 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

We can equivalently write these hypotheses in terms of the difference \(p_1 - p_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Case 1: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p_1 - p_2 \leq 0 \\ H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0 \end{cases} \qquad \text{Case 2: } \begin{cases} H_0: p_1 - p_2 \geq 0 \\ H_a: p_1 - p_2 < 0 \end{cases} \\ \\ \text{Case 3: } &\begin{cases} H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0 \\ H_a: p_1 - p_2 \neq 0 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

Cases 1 and 2 are one-sided tests, while Case 3 is a two-sided test. In practice, the choice of case depends on the research question—specifically, whether we are testing for an increase, a decrease, or any difference. Let’s see how to set up the hypotheses for each of our examples.

Example 9.35 (Schema-Based Instruction: Stating Hypotheses) Researchers want to know whether a new schema-based instructional approach improves seventh-grade students’ mastery of proportional reasoning compared to traditional instruction. They randomly assign \(n_1=50\) students to the new approach and \(n_2=50\) students to traditional instruction. Let \(p_1\) represent the proportion of all students who would achieve mastery under the new approach, and \(p_2\) represent the proportion who would achieve mastery under traditional instruction. State the appropriate null and alternative hypotheses to address the research question.

🔎 Solution

Since the researchers want to know whether the new approach improves mastery (that is, whether \(p_1 > p_2\)), this is a Case 1 situation. The hypotheses are:

\[ \begin{cases} H_0: p_1 \leq p_2 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad H_0: p_1 - p_2 \leq 0\\ H_a: p_1 > p_2 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0 \end{cases} \]

The null hypothesis states that the new approach is no better than (or is worse than) traditional instruction. The alternative hypothesis states that the new approach produces a higher mastery rate.

Example 9.36 (Text Message Reminders: Stating Hypotheses) Researchers are piloting a text message reminder system for diabetes patients. They want to know whether daily text reminders improve medication adherence compared to standard care, but they’re also open to the possibility that the reminders might perform worse (perhaps by being annoying or overwhelming). \[ \begin{cases} H_0: p_1 = p_2 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\\ H_a: p_1 \neq p_2 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad H_a: p_1 - p_2 \neq 0 \end{cases} \]

The null hypothesis states that the difference between the two population proportions, \(p_1\) and \(p_2,\) is zero. A difference of zero means that the two population proportions are equal, so there is no difference in adherence rates between the two approaches. 9.7.2 Data, Statistic, and Sampling Distribution

To make inferences about \(p_1 - p_2,\) we collect a sample of size \(n_1\) from population 1 and a sample of size \(n_2\) from population 2. Note that these sample sizes don’t have to be equal—researchers often work with unequal group sizes due to practical constraints or deliberate design choices.

Let \(X_1\) denote the number of successes (favorable outcomes) in our sample from population 1, and let \(X_2\) denote the number of successes in our sample from population 2. Although we could compare the counts through \(X_1-X_2,\) differences in counts are difficult to analyze and can be misleading when the sample sizes differ. Instead, we compare sample proportions and rely on the Central Limit Theorem for large samples.

The sample proportions are \(\hat{p}_1 = X_1/n_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2 = X_2/n_2.\) These are our point estimates of \(p_1\) and \(p_2,\) respectively. To make inferences about the difference \(p_1 - p_2,\) we naturally use the difference in sample proportions: \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2.\)

The Sampling Distribution of \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\)

When the sample sizes \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are both sufficiently large, the Central Limit Theorem tells us that the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\) is approximately normal. The rule of thumb for “sufficiently large” is that each group must have at least 10 successes and 10 failures: \(n_1\hat{p}_1 \geq 10,\) \(n_1(1-\hat{p}_1) \geq 10,\) \(n_2\hat{p}_2 \geq 10,\) and \(n_2(1-\hat{p}_2) \geq 10.\)

When these conditions are met, the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\) is approximately normal with mean \(p_1 - p_2\) and standard error

\[ \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \sim N\left(p_1 - p_2, \; \text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)\right), \] where \[ \text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}. \]

Notice that the variability depends on both sample sizes and population proportions.

Simplification Under the Null Hypothesis

Recall that when testing hypotheses, we work at the boundary of the null hypothesis, since the Type I error is maximized there. For all three cases above, the boundary value is \(p_1 - p_2 = 0,\) which means \(p_1 = p_2.\)

At this boundary, the two population proportions are equal. We denote their common value by \(p,\) so that \(p_1=p_2=p.\) As a result, the standard error simplifies to:

\[ \text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n_1} + \frac{p(1-p)}{n_2}} = \sqrt{p(1-p)\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}. \]

Therefore, under the null hypothesis, the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\) is:

\[ \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \sim N\left(0, \; \sqrt{p(1-p)\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}\right). \]

This is the distribution we will use for hypothesis testing.

Estimating the Common Proportion

There is one practical issue: we do not know \(p,\) the common population proportion under \(H_0.\) Because \(H_0\) assumes that \(p_1=p_2,\) we treat the two samples as coming from populations with the same proportion. We therefore combine the number of successes from both samples and divide by the combined sample size. This gives us an estimate of the common population proportion called the pooled sample proportion: \[\hat{p} = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{n_1 + n_2}.\]

Using \(\hat{p}\) in place of \(p\) gives the estimated standard error:

\[ \widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}. \]

This estimated standard error is used in our test statistic and in determining critical regions and p-values, just as we did in the one-population case.

  1. Compute the sample proportions \(\hat{p}_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2\) and their difference.
  2. Verify the large sample conditions.
  3. Compute the pooled sample proportion \(\hat{p}.\)
  4. Compute the estimated standard error of \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2.\)
🔎 Solution

a. Sample proportions.

\[\hat{p}_1 = \frac{34}{50} = 0.68, \qquad \hat{p}_2 = \frac{24}{50} = 0.48, \qquad \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = 0.20.\]

The new approach shows a 20 percentage point higher mastery rate in the sample. We will test whether this provides sufficient evidence that \(p_1 > p_2\) or whether it could reasonably arise by chance when both approaches are equally effective.

b. Large sample conditions.

We check whether each group has at least 10 successes and 10 failures.

For Group 1 (new approach):

\[n_1 \hat{p}_1 = 50 \times 0.68 = 34, \qquad n_1(1 - \hat{p}_1) = 50 \times 0.32 = 16.\]

Under the null hypothesis, we estimate that 58% of students would achieve mastery if both approaches were equally effective.

9.7.3 Hypothesis Testing: p-Value and Critical Region Approaches

As we explained earlier, there are two equivalent approaches to making decisions in hypothesis testing: the p-value approach and the critical region approach. We now apply both approaches to testing hypotheses about the difference between two population proportions, \(p_1-p_2,\) and show how to carry them out using Rguroo.

Recall that we developed the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2\) at the null boundary \(p_1-p_2=0\) in the previous section. When the large-sample conditions are satisfied, we use this approximately normal distribution, together with the pooled sample proportion, to carry out the test. Both the p-value and critical region approaches use this same sampling distribution to determine whether the observed difference \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\) provides sufficient evidence against \(H_0\) and favors \(H_a.\)

A Note on Notation

In the one-sample sections, we used \(\hat{p}_{\text{obs}}\) to distinguish the observed sample proportion from the random variable \(\hat{p}.\) For two proportions, that notation becomes cumbersome — we would need \(\hat{p}_{1,\text{obs}}\) and \(\hat{p}_{2,\text{obs}}.\) To keep the notation simpler, we use \(\hat{p}_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2\) for both meanings. The context will make the meaning clear: when discussing sampling distributions or probabilities, they represent random variables; when working with the observed data or making a decision, they represent the observed sample proportions.

The p-value approach asks: “How much support does the observed difference provide for \(H_a\)?” The critical region approach asks: “Does the observed difference fall in the critical region?” Both approaches lead to the same conclusion, and we will use Rguroo to carry out both approaches.

Let’s examine each case in detail.

9.7.3.1 Case 1: Right-Tailed Test (\(H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0\))

In this case, we want to test whether \(p_1 > p_2.\) The alternative hypothesis \(H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0\) is supported when \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\) is positive, with larger positive differences providing stronger support for \(H_a.\) Values near zero or negative provide little or no support for \(H_a.\)

p-Value Approach:

The p-value measures he strength of the evidence against \(H_0\) and in favor of \(H_a.\) For a right-tailed test, the p-value is the probability of observing a difference that provides at least as much support for \(H_a\) as our observed difference, assuming \(p_1=p_2\):

\[\text{p-value} = P\left(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2 \geq \text{observed difference}\right),\; \text{assuming } p_1=p_2.\]

This is the area to the right of the observed difference under the \(N\left(0, \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}\right)\) distribution. A small p-value indicates strong evidence against \(H_0\) and in favor of \(H_a,\) while a large p-value indicates that the data do not provide strong evidence against \(H_0.\)

Decision Rule:

  • Reject \(H_0\) if p-value \(< \alpha\) (observed difference strongly support \(H_a\)).
  • Fail to reject \(H_0\) if p-value \(\geq \alpha\) (data do not strongly support \(H_a\)).

Critical Region Approach:

The critical region consists of values in the upper tail of the sampling distribution. These are the values of \(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2\) that are sufficiently large to lead us to reject \(H_0\) in favor of \(H_a: p_1-p_2>0.\) We find the critical value \(c\) o that the probability of falling in the critical region is equal to the significant level \(\alpha,\) assuming \(p_1=p_2\):

\[P\left(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2 \geq c\right) = \alpha,\; \text{assuming }p_1=p_2.\]

Using the sampling distribution under this assumption,

\[\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2\sim N\left(0, \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}\right)\]

we choose \(c\) so that the area to the right of \(c\) is \(\alpha.\) We reject \(H_0\) if the observed difference \(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2\) falls in this critical region.

Decision Rule:

  • Reject \(H_0\) if \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 > c\) (observed difference strongly supports \(H_a\)).
  • Fail to reject \(H_0\) if \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \leq c\) (observed difference does not strongly support \(H_a\)).

  • Fail to reject \(H_0\) if \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \geq c\) (observed difference does not strongly support \(H_a\)).

9.8 Confidence Interval for the Difference Between Two Population Proportions

In Section 9.7, we learned how to test whether two population proportions differ. For example, in the schema-based instruction study (Example 9.40), we found strong evidence that the new instructional approach produced higher mastery rates than traditional instruction. The hypothesis test told us that a difference exists, but it didn’t tell us how large that difference might be. Does the new approach improve mastery by 5 percentage points? 20 percentage points? 35 percentage points? The hypothesis test alone cannot answer this question.

This is where confidence intervals become valuable. A confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) provides a range of plausible values for the true difference between the two proportions, addressing the question “How large is the difference?” It tells us not just that a difference exists, but also gives us insight into the magnitude and direction of that difference. For instance, a confidence interval might tell us: “We are 95% confident that the new approach improves mastery rates by somewhere between 4 and 36 percentage points compared to traditional instruction.” This additional information helps researchers, educators, and policymakers make more informed decisions.

Just as in the one-population case (Section 9.6), a confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) can be derived by inverting hypothesis tests: given the observed difference \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2,\) the \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) interval consists of all values \(d\) for which we would fail to reject \(H_0: p_1 - p_2 = d\) in a two-sided test at significance level \(\alpha.\) And as in the one-population case, the confidence level describes the long-run behavior of the construction method, not any single interval — a 95% method captures the true difference in 95% of repeated samples. In practice we use a direct formula based on the normal approximation rather than carrying out the test inversion by hand, but the logic is the same.

9.8.1 Large Sample Confidence Interval for \(p_1 - p_2\)

When the large sample conditions are satisfied, we can construct a confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) using the normal approximation. Recall from Section 9.7.2 that when both samples are sufficiently large, the difference in sample proportions \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\) follows an approximately normal distribution.

However, there is an important difference between constructing a confidence interval and conducting a hypothesis test. In hypothesis testing, we assumed \(H_0: p_1 = p_2\) was true, which allowed us to use a pooled estimate \(\hat{p}\) for the common proportion. When constructing a confidence interval, we do not assume the proportions are equal—in fact, we’re trying to estimate how different they are! Therefore, we cannot use the pooled proportion.

Instead, for a confidence interval, we estimate the standard error using the individual sample proportions \(\hat{p}_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2\):

\[\text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}.\]

This is called the unpooled standard error, or simply the standard error, because we do not pool the two samples together.

Pooled vs. Unpooled Standard Error

The two-proportion confidence interval uses the unpooled standard error, based on \(\hat{p}_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2\) separately: \[\text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}.\]

The two-proportion hypothesis test, by contrast, uses the pooled standard error, based on the common proportion \(\hat{p}\) under \(H_0: p_1 = p_2\) (see Section 9.7.2): \[\widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}.\]

The reason is the assumption being made. The hypothesis test assumes \(p_1 = p_2,\) so we pool. The confidence interval makes no such assumption — we’re trying to estimate the difference, not assume it away — so we estimate each proportion’s variance from its own sample.

The large sample conditions for a confidence interval are the same as those for hypothesis testing. We need at least 10 successes and at least 10 failures in each group:

\[n_1\hat{p}_1 \geq 10, \quad n_1(1-\hat{p}_1) \geq 10, \quad n_2\hat{p}_2 \geq 10, \quad n_2(1-\hat{p}_2) \geq 10.\]

When these conditions are satisfied, the \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) takes the form:

\[(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z^* \times \text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2),\]

where \(z^*\) is the critical value from the standard normal distribution corresponding to the desired confidence level, just as in the one-population case.

Large Sample Confidence Interval for \(p_1 - p_2\)

When the large sample conditions are satisfied, an approximate \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) is:

\[(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm \underbrace{z^* \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}}_{\text{margin of error}}.\]

That is, the lower endpoint is \((\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) - z^* \cdot \text{SE}\) and the upper endpoint is \((\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) + z^* \cdot \text{SE},\) where \(\text{SE}\) is the unpooled standard error given above.

Common critical values remain the same:

Confidence Level \(\alpha\) \(z^*\)
90% 0.10 1.645
95% 0.05 1.960
99% 0.01 2.576

9.8.2 Relationship Between Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Tests

There is an important connection between confidence intervals and two-sided hypothesis tests. A \((1-\alpha)\times100%\) confidence interval and a two-sided hypothesis test at significance level \(\alpha\) lead to the same decisions about the null value \(p_1-p_2=0\):

  • If a \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) does not contain 0, then 0 is not a plausible value for \(p_1-p_2,\) and we reject \(H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\) at significance level \(\alpha\) in a two-sided test.

  • If a \((1-\alpha) \times 100\%\) confidence interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) contains 0, 0 is a plausible value for \(p_1-p_2,\) and we fail to reject \(H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\) at significance level \(\alpha\) in a two-sided test.

This relationship provides a quick way to connect the confidence interval to the corresponding two-sided hypothesis test. As noted, however, confidence intervals provide more information than hypothesis tests alone. They tell us not just whether the data provide evidence of a difference, but also which values are plausible for the size and direction of the difference.

This direct relationship applies only to two-sided hypothesis tests. For a one-sided hypothesis test, you should not use this confidence interval rule to determine whether to reject \(H_0.\)

a. We check the large sample conditions using the individual sample proportions:

  • Group 1 (females): \(n_1\hat{p}_1 = 204 \times 0.696 \approx 142 \geq 10\) and \(n_1(1-\hat{p}_1) = 204 \times 0.304 \approx 62 \geq 10\)

All conditions are comfortably satisfied, the large-sample normal approximation is appropriate for constructing the confidence interval.

Note that the observed difference was \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 \approx -0.065,\) meaning the sample showed about 6.5 percentage points lower support among females.
